\section{Introduction}
Dispersive (frequency--selective) channels smear each transmitted symbol across multiple sampling intervals, producing inter--symbol interference (ISI) that rotates and compresses the constellation and degrades symbol error rate (SER). Such conditions arise in wireline links with impedance mismatches and reflections (e.g., copper, coax) as well as in wireless links with multipath delay spread. Equalization is therefore a central receiver function that attempts to undo the channel's memory and restore reliable decisions at the detector.

Classical solutions span linear and nonlinear strategies. Linear time--invariant equalizers include the zero--forcing (ZF) and minimum mean--square error (MMSE) designs; ZF removes ISI at the cost of noise enhancement, whereas MMSE trades residual ISI for reduced noise amplification \cite{proakis}. Nonlinear receivers, notably decision--feedback equalizers (DFE), cancel postcursor ISI more effectively by leveraging past decisions, and maximum likelihood sequence estimation (MLSE) achieves optimal sequence detection at substantially higher complexity \cite{proakis}. Since channel statistics and propagation conditions vary across time, practical receivers employ \emph{adaptive} algorithms to track the optimum equalizer in situ. Early adaptive equalization work (e.g., Lucky) and comprehensive surveys (e.g., Qureshi) established the foundational architectures and operating modes (training vs.\ decision--directed) used today \cite{lucky,qureshi}. Among adaptive algorithms, least mean squares (LMS) is particularly attractive for its simplicity, modest computational load, and robustness to modeling uncertainties; it performs stochastic gradient descent with a single step--size parameter that governs the speed/misadjustment trade--off \cite{haykin,qureshi}. Variants such as normalized LMS (NLMS), recursive least squares (RLS), and blind methods like the constant modulus algorithm (CMA) further broaden the design space \cite{haykin,qureshi}.

\paragraph{Project scope and context.}
This project implements and evaluates an LMS--based, decision--directed adaptive FIR \emph{linear} equalizer for 4--QAM transmission over frequency--selective channels with complex AWGN. The simulation (in MATLAB) reflects a standard baseband receiver: a known training preamble initializes adaptation, after which the loop switches to decision--directed (DD) operation for continued tracking \cite{qureshi,haykin}. Channels are modeled as unit--energy FIR responses to cleanly separate ISI effects from SNR scaling. This project consider three representative channels that span typical use cases: a mild 3--tap profile, a severe 5--tap profile with pre/postcursor ISI, and a random Rayleigh multipath profile (all normalized to unit energy). The equalizer employs an automatically chosen decision delay aligned to the dominant channel tap (a common heuristic in adaptive linear equalization) and measures both learning behavior and decision performance.

\paragraph{Objectives.}
Guided by the theory in \cite{haykin,proakis} and the adaptive equalization practices summarized in \cite{qureshi,lucky}, the project pursues four concrete goals:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Build a reproducible end--to--end 4--QAM simulation including channel, AWGN, training, decision device, and a LMS adaptive FIR equalizer.
  \item Implement two operating modes---training and decision--directed---with an automatic decision delay selection tied to the channel impulse response.
  \item Quantify performance via (i) learning curves $|e[n]|^2$ that expose convergence speed and steady--state misadjustment and (ii) symbol error rate (SER) measured in DD mode.
  \item Study key design knobs and trade--offs predicted by theory: SNR, equalizer length $N$ (span vs.\ complexity/misadjustment), and LMS step size $\mu$ (convergence speed vs.\ misadjustment/stability).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions and takeaways.}
Within this scope, the report (i) provides a MATLAB reference implementation for LMS equalization with training and DD operation; (ii) connects empirical behavior (learning curves, SER, constellations) to LMS theory---including step--size stability bounds and misadjustment scaling with $N$ and received power \cite{haykin}; and (iii) identifies practical settings of $(N,\mu)$ that balance convergence and steady--state error on channels with different ISI profiles.

% ------------------------------ 2. MODEL ---------------------------------
\section{System Model and Problem Formulation}\label{sec:model}
This section provides mathematical descriptions of the adaptive equalization system, with each component corresponding to the block diagram in Fig.~\ref{fig:block}.

\subsection{Transmitter and Signal Generation}
The data generator produces an independent and identically distributed (IID) sequence of information symbols $x[n]$ drawn from a 4--QAM constellation $S$. The constellation consists of four equi--spaced points in the complex plane:
\begin{equation}
S = \left\{s_1, s_2, s_3, s_4\right\} = \frac{\sigma_s}{\sqrt{2}}\left\{1+j, -1+j, -1-j, 1-j\right\},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_s^2$ is the symbol power, ensuring $E\{|x[n]|^2\} = \sigma_s^2$. Each symbol is selected with equal probability $P(x[n]=s_i) = 1/4$ for $i=1,\ldots,4$. In the simulation, symbols are generated by mapping random indices to these constellation points.

\subsection{Channel Model and Inter--Symbol Interference}
The channel filter $h[n]$ models the combined effect of transmit filtering, physical propagation (multipath, reflections), and receive filtering. It is represented as a finite impulse response (FIR) filter of length $K$:
\begin{equation}
h[n] = \begin{cases} h_n & \text{for } n=0,1,\ldots,K-1 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The channel is normalized to unit energy such that $\sum_{n=0}^{K-1}|h[n]|^2 = 1$, so it does not amplify or attenuate the average signal power.

The simulation considers three representative channel types:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Mild ISI:} A 3--tap channel $h = [0.9+0j, 0.3-0.2j, 0.15+0.05j]$ with modest postcursor interference.
\item \textbf{Severe ISI:} A 5--tap channel $h = [0.3+0.2j, 0.9+0j, 0.25-0.15j, 0.15+0.10j, 0.05-0.06j]$ with both precursor and postcursor interference.
\item \textbf{Random Rayleigh:} A $K$--tap channel with complex Gaussian taps $h[k] \sim \mathcal{CN}(0, \sigma_h^2)$ before normalization, representing a frequency--selective fading channel.
\end{itemize}

The channel output before noise addition is the convolution
\begin{equation}
y_{\text{clean}}[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} h[k]\,x[n-k] = (h*x)[n],
\end{equation}
which introduces inter--symbol interference (ISI) when $K > 1$, as each received sample contains contributions from multiple transmitted symbols.

\subsection{Additive Noise Model}
The noise generator produces complex circularly symmetric additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) $w[n] \sim \mathcal{CN}(0, \sigma_n^2)$, where $\sigma_n^2$ is chosen to achieve a target signal--to--noise ratio (SNR) defined as
\begin{equation}
\text{SNR}_{\text{dB}} = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_n^2}\right).
\end{equation}
Since the channel is unit energy, $E\{|y_{\text{clean}}[n]|^2\} \approx \sigma_s^2$, and the noise variance is computed as $\sigma_n^2 = \sigma_s^2 / 10^{\text{SNR}_{\text{dB}}/10}$. The final received signal at the equalizer input is
\begin{equation}
y[n] = y_{\text{clean}}[n] + w[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} h[k]\,x[n-k] + w[n],
\end{equation}
which exhibits both ISI distortion and noise corruption.

\subsection{Adaptive Equalizer Structure}
The adaptive equalizer is an $N$--tap complex FIR filter with time--varying coefficients $\mathbf{c}[n] = [c_0[n], c_1[n], \ldots, c_{N-1}[n]]^T$. At each time $n$, it forms a linear combination of the $N$ most recent received samples:
\begin{equation}
\hat{x}[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} c_k[n]\,y[n-k] = \mathbf{c}^H[n]\,\mathbf{y}[n],
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{y}[n] = [y[n], y[n-1], \ldots, y[n-N+1]]^T$ is the input regressor vector. The equalizer output $\hat{x}[n]$ is a soft estimate of the transmitted symbol.

\subsection{Decision Device and Symbol Detection}
The decision device implements minimum--distance detection, mapping the equalizer output $\hat{x}[n]$ to the nearest constellation point:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{x}[n] = \mathcal{Q}(\hat{x}[n]) = \arg\min_{s \in S} |\hat{x}[n] - s|^2.
\end{equation}
That is, the detector computes distances to all four 4--QAM points and selects the symbol with minimum Euclidean distance.

\subsection{Training and Decision--Directed Modes}
The system operates in two distinct phases, controlled by a switch in the block diagram (Fig.~\ref{fig:block}):

\textbf{Training Phase:} During the initial $L_{\text{tr}}$ symbols, the transmitter sends a known training sequence. The desired signal for the equalizer is set to $d[n] = x[n-D]$, where $D$ is the \emph{decision delay}. This delay accounts for the fact that the equalizer output $\hat{x}[n]$ estimates a symbol transmitted $D$ samples earlier due to the channel's memory. The delay $D$ is chosen to align the equalizer's main tap with the dominant channel tap, by selecting the index of the largest $|h[k]|$ and mapping it to an appropriate equalizer tap.

\textbf{Decision--Directed Phase:} After training, the system switches to decision--directed mode, where the desired signal becomes
\begin{equation}
d[n] = \tilde{x}[n] = \mathcal{Q}(\hat{x}[n]).
\end{equation}
This allows the equalizer to continue adapting to track slow channel variations without requiring additional training overhead.

\subsection{Error Signal and Adaptation}
The error signal is computed as the difference between the desired and actual equalizer output:
\begin{equation}
e[n] = d[n] - \hat{x}[n],
\end{equation}
where $d[n]$ switches between $x[n-D]$ (training) and $\tilde{x}[n]$ (decision--directed) as described above. This error signal drives the LMS adaptation algorithm, which updates the equalizer coefficients to minimize the mean squared error $E\{|e[n]|^2\}$.

\subsection{Performance Metrics}
The system performance is evaluated using two primary metrics:

\textbf{Learning Curve:} The instantaneous squared error $|e[n]|^2$ versus time $n$, which reveals the convergence behavior during training and the steady--state residual error. In the simulations, this curve is often smoothed and averaged over multiple Monte Carlo trials to approximate $E\{|e[n]|^2\}$.

\textbf{Symbol Error Rate (SER):} The probability of symbol detection error, computed as
\begin{equation}
\text{SER} = \frac{1}{L_{\text{data}}} \sum_{n=n_0}^{L} \mathbf{1}\{\tilde{x}[n] \neq x[n-D]\},
\end{equation}
where $n_0$ marks the start of the decision--directed phase and $L_{\text{data}}$ is the number of data symbols. The SER is measured only during decision--directed operation and averaged over multiple independent realizations.

\section{Adaptive LMS Equalization}\label{sec:lms}
This section reviews the least mean squares (LMS) algorithm for adaptive equalization, including its mathematical formulation, implementation, and design trade--offs for parameter selection.

\subsection{LMS Algorithm and Cost Function}
The LMS algorithm is a stochastic gradient descent method that minimizes the mean squared error (MSE) cost function:
\begin{equation}
J(\mathbf{c}) = E\{|e[n]|^2\} = E\{|d[n] - \mathbf{c}^H\mathbf{y}[n]|^2\},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{y}[n] = [y[n], y[n-1], \ldots, y[n-N+1]]^T$ is the input regressor vector and $\mathbf{c} = [c_0, c_1, \ldots, c_{N-1}]^T$ is the equalizer coefficient vector. Using an instantaneous gradient approximation, the LMS update takes the form~\cite{haykin}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{c}[n+1] = \mathbf{c}[n] + \mu \mathbf{y}[n]e^*[n],
\end{equation}
where $\mu > 0$ is the step size parameter and $e[n] = d[n] - \mathbf{c}^H[n]\mathbf{y}[n]$ is the error signal. This update adapts the equalizer coefficients at each time step using only the current input vector and error.

\subsection{Algorithm Implementation}
The LMS equalization algorithm proceeds as follows.

\textbf{Initialization:} The equalizer coefficients are initialized as a delayed delta function:
\begin{equation}
c_k[0] = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } k = D \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases},
\end{equation}
where $D$ is the decision delay. This places the equalizer's main tap at position $D$, providing a reasonable starting point that forwards the $D$--th delayed sample and accelerates convergence compared to zero initialization.

\textbf{Main Loop:} For each time index $n$ (after enough samples are available to form $\mathbf{y}[n]$):

\begin{enumerate}
\item Form the input vector $\mathbf{y}[n] = [y[n], y[n-1], \ldots, y[n-N+1]]^T$.
\item Compute the equalizer output $\hat{x}[n] = \mathbf{c}^H[n]\mathbf{y}[n]$.
\item Determine the desired signal:
\begin{equation}
d[n] = \begin{cases}
x[n-D] & \text{training mode} \\
\mathcal{Q}(\hat{x}[n]) & \text{decision--directed mode}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
\item Compute the error $e[n] = d[n] - \hat{x}[n]$.
\item Update the coefficient vector via $\mathbf{c}[n+1] = \mathbf{c}[n] + \mu \mathbf{y}[n]e^*[n]$.
\end{enumerate}

This loop produces the equalized output, the error sequence, and the time evolution of the coefficients, which are later used to analyze convergence and steady--state behavior.

\subsection{Convergence Properties}
The convergence properties of LMS depend critically on the step size $\mu$. For convergence in the mean, the step size must satisfy~\cite{haykin}:
\begin{equation}
0 < \mu < \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\max}$ is the largest eigenvalue of the input correlation matrix $\mathbf{R}_y = E\{\mathbf{y}[n]\mathbf{y}^H[n]\}$. A more practical bound uses the trace of $\mathbf{R}_y$:
\begin{equation}
0 < \mu < \frac{2}{\mathrm{tr}\{\mathbf{R}_y\}} = \frac{2}{N P_y},
\end{equation}
where $P_y = E\{|y[n]|^2\}$ is the average received power. For a unit--energy channel, $P_y \approx \sigma_s^2 + \sigma_n^2$, which leads to the rule of thumb
\begin{equation}
\mu \lesssim \frac{1}{N(\sigma_s^2 + \sigma_n^2)}.
\end{equation}

The steady--state excess MSE (misadjustment) is approximately
\begin{equation}
M = \frac{\mu}{2} \mathrm{tr}\{\mathbf{R}_y\} = \frac{\mu N P_y}{2},
\end{equation}
which increases linearly with $\mu$ and $N$. Thus, larger $\mu$ accelerates convergence but increases steady--state error and the risk of instability, while smaller $\mu$ reduces misadjustment at the cost of slower convergence.

\subsection{Parameter Selection and Design Trade--Offs}

\textbf{Equalizer Length $N$:} The equalizer must span the channel's delay spread to effectively cancel ISI. If the channel has significant energy over $K$ taps, the equalizer length should satisfy $N \gtrsim K$ to capture the channel memory. Choosing $N$ too large increases per--iteration complexity, slows convergence, and raises misadjustment ($M \propto \mu N P_y$), and may lead to over--fitting noise. In practice, $N$ is chosen as the smallest value that spans the significant channel energy, typically around $K$ to $2K$ taps.

\textbf{Step Size $\mu$:} The step size is the most critical parameter, balancing convergence speed and steady--state performance. Larger $\mu$ reduces the convergence time, which is important when the training length $L_{\text{tr}}$ is limited, but increases the steady--state misadjustment and may cause instability, especially in decision--directed mode. Smaller $\mu$ improves steady--state MSE and SER but may not allow the equalizer to converge fully within the training window. In the simulations, $\mu$ is chosen according to the above rule of thumb, and then refined empirically.

\textbf{Decision Delay $D$:} The decision delay aligns the desired symbol $x[n-D]$ with the equalizer’s estimate $\hat{x}[n]$. A simple and effective heuristic is to choose $D$ to align the equalizer’s main tap with the dominant channel tap (the index of the largest $|h[k]|$). This tends to maximize the signal--to--interference ratio at the equalizer output and simplifies initialization.

\textbf{Training Length $L_{\text{tr}}$:} The training duration must be sufficient for the equalizer to approach steady--state. The required length depends on the convergence time constant, which scales inversely with $\mu$ and the smallest eigenvalue of $\mathbf{R}_y$. A rough rule of thumb is to choose $L_{\text{tr}}$ several times larger than the convergence time constant so that the transition to decision--directed mode occurs after the main transient has decayed.

\subsection{Practical Considerations}
In the MATLAB implementation, care is taken to handle filter transients, maintain consistent constellation scaling between modulator and detector, and avoid array boundary issues when indexing $x[n-D]$ and $\mathbf{y}[n]$. These implementation details do not change the theoretical formulation above but are important for obtaining stable and reproducible simulation results.

\section{Results and Discussion}\label{sec:results}
In this report, results are presented for a representative configuration selected through a comprehensive benchmark test. The optimal parameters identified are an $N=11$ tap equalizer, step size $\mu=0.003$, the ``severe'' 5--tap channel normalized to unit energy, a training length of $L_{\text{tr}}=1500$ symbols followed by $15000$ payload symbols, and decision delay chosen automatically from the dominant channel tap. The benchmark test evaluated performance at \SI{30}{dB} SNR across a grid of $\mu$ values ($0.0005$ to $0.015$) and equalizer lengths ($N=5$ to $17$). Reported curves are averaged over multiple Monte Carlo trials except where individual realizations are explicitly shown.

\subsection{Constellation Diagrams and Learning Curve}
Figure~\ref{fig:constellations} presents constellation diagrams before and after equalization for a single run at \SI{30}{dB} SNR. The left subplot shows the received signal $y[n]$ at the equalizer input. Because of ISI and noise, the four ideal 4--QAM points have been smeared into broad, significantly overlapping clouds with a dense central region; the decision boundaries between symbols are not visually apparent. The right subplot shows the equalizer output $\hat{x}[n]$ in steady state. The four symbol clusters are clearly pulled back toward the nominal 4--QAM locations and become much more compact, with a visible ``hole'' around the origin. Some residual spread remains around each point, which is consistent with the AWGN level and LMS misadjustment. This visual comparison confirms that the LMS equalizer reshapes the distorted input distribution into one that is much closer to the ideal constellation, enabling reliable minimum--distance detection.

Figure~\ref{fig:learning} shows the corresponding learning curve for the same run, plotting a smoothed version of $|e[n]|^2$ in decibels as a function of time index $n$. The error power starts around $+1$ to \SI{2}{dB} and falls rapidly during the first few hundred symbols, reaching roughly \SI{-4.5}{dB} by $n \approx 500$. After that, the curve continues to decrease more slowly and approaches a steady level near \SI{-7}{dB}. The vertical dashed line at $n = 1500$ marks the transition from training to decision--directed mode. Importantly, there is no noticeable jump at this point: the mean error level remains essentially unchanged and only the variability increases slightly due to occasional decision errors in the decision--directed phase. The horizontal dashed line indicates the steady--state level estimated from the last 100 samples. The observed residual MSE is in line with LMS theory, which predicts a nonzero misadjustment proportional to $\mu N P_y$, together with the irreducible noise floor set by $\sigma_n^2$.

\subsection{SER versus SNR}
Figure~\ref{fig:ser_snr} shows the symbol error rate as a function of SNR for the severe 5--tap channel, with $N=11$ and $\mu=0.003$. Each point is obtained by averaging SER over 5 independent realizations. The SER decreases monotonically from about $3\times 10^{-1}$ at \SI{5}{dB} to slightly above $10^{-2}$ at \SI{30}{dB}. When plotted on a logarithmic scale, the curve is approximately straight, indicating an exponential dependence on SNR, but the slope is moderate: a \SI{10}{dB} increase in SNR reduces SER by roughly a factor of 2--3 rather than a full decade. Within the explored SNR range the curve continues to fall and does not exhibit a clear error floor, suggesting that performance remains primarily noise--limited for this choice of equalizer length and step size. At higher SNRs one would expect the SER to eventually saturate to a floor determined by residual ISI and LMS misadjustment, but this regime is not reached in the present simulations.

\subsection{Impact of Step Size $\mu$ and Equalizer Length $N$}

Figure~\ref{fig:ser_mu} illustrates the effect of the LMS step size on performance at fixed SNR \SI{30}{dB} for $N=11$. For very small step sizes (e.g., $\mu = 5\times 10^{-4}$), the equalizer adapts so slowly that it cannot converge within the $1500$--symbol training window; the resulting SER is close to $0.5$, i.e., only slightly better than random guessing. As $\mu$ increases toward $10^{-3}$, the SER improves dramatically. There is a broad plateau of near--optimal performance for $\mu$ between approximately $0.003$ and $0.005$, where SER is on the order of $2\times 10^{-2}$. For larger $\mu$ values up to $0.01$, misadjustment becomes more significant and the SER starts to increase again, but the degradation is modest in this range. This behavior clearly illustrates the classical trade--off: very small $\mu$ leads to under--adaptation, while very large $\mu$ increases steady--state error and sensitivity to decision errors; between these extremes there is a relatively wide region where performance is robust to the exact choice of $\mu$.

Figure~\ref{fig:ser_n} shows the dependence of SER on the equalizer length $N$ at \SI{30}{dB} SNR with $\mu=0.003$. For this 5--tap channel, SER values lie between about $2.6\times 10^{-2}$ and $3.1\times 10^{-2}$ for all tested lengths, so the dependence on $N$ is relatively weak once the equalizer is long enough to span the channel memory. The best performance in this set is obtained with $N=7$, which is only slightly longer than the channel. Increasing $N$ to 9, 11, or 13 taps does not significantly change SER, indicating that these filters all have sufficient span to capture the dominant ISI. At $N=15$ the SER rises somewhat, consistent with the increase in misadjustment and slower convergence that occur when more coefficients must be adapted within the same training interval. These results support the common design guideline that $N$ should be chosen just large enough to cover the effective spread of the channel, with little benefit from much longer equalizers in the linear LMS setting.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
This project implemented and evaluated a complete 4--QAM transmission system with an adaptive LMS linear equalizer operating over frequency–selective channels. Using a realistic simulation chain—including controlled ISI, AWGN, a training sequence, and decision–directed tracking—the study examined how the equalizer reshapes distorted constellations, how the LMS algorithm converges in practice, and how symbol error rate varies with SNR, step size, and filter length.

The results confirm that a properly tuned LMS equalizer can substantially mitigate ISI: the distorted, overlapping input constellation is transformed into four well-separated clusters suitable for reliable detection, and the learning curve shows rapid initial convergence followed by stable decision-directed operation. SER decreases monotonically with SNR across the tested range, remaining noise-limited without exhibiting a strong error floor for the chosen parameters. Parameter sweeps highlight the expected LMS trade-offs: very small step sizes fail to converge within the available training, while very large values suffer from misadjustment; a broad intermediate region provides robust performance. Equalizer-length experiments further show that once $N$ exceeds the channel memory, SER becomes relatively insensitive to additional taps, with excessively long equalizers yielding slightly worse performance due to slower convergence and increased misadjustment.

Overall, the study reinforces classical adaptive filter theory in a practical communication scenario and demonstrates how design choices such as $N$, $\mu$, and training duration must be balanced to achieve stable and effective equalization. The provided MATLAB framework can be extended to explore more advanced techniques such as decision–feedback equalization, normalized LMS, or blind initialization methods like CMA.

\appendix
\section*{Appendix: MATLAB Code Organization}
This appendix describes the MATLAB source files and their purpose. All files are located in the \texttt{matlab/} directory.

\subsection*{Main Scripts}
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{main.m}: Main driver script that orchestrates the complete simulation. Configures system parameters (SNR, equalizer length $N$, step size $\mu$, training length), generates the channel, runs Monte Carlo trials, and produces all performance plots including learning curves, SER versus SNR, SER versus $\mu$, and SER versus $N$.
\item \texttt{simulate\_one\_run.m}: Executes a single Monte Carlo realization of the transmission system. Generates random 4--QAM symbols, passes them through the channel with AWGN, applies the LMS equalizer, and computes the symbol error rate. Returns all internal signals for analysis and plotting.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Equalization Core}
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{lms\_equalizer.m}: Implements the LMS adaptive FIR equalizer. Performs the core adaptation loop: computes equalizer output $\hat{x}[n]$, determines the desired signal $d[n]$ (training or decision--directed), computes the error $e[n]$, and updates coefficients via $\mathbf{c}[n+1] = \mathbf{c}[n] + \mu \mathbf{y}[n]e^*[n]$. Returns the equalized output, error signal, coefficient history, and MSE learning curve.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Modulation and Demodulation}
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{qam4\_constellation.m}: Defines the 4--QAM constellation points $S = \frac{\sigma_s}{\sqrt{2}}[1+j, -1+j, -1-j, 1-j]$ with configurable power scaling.
\item \texttt{qam4\_mod.m}: Maps integer indices $\{1,2,3,4\}$ to 4--QAM constellation symbols for transmission.
\item \texttt{qam4\_demod.m}: Implements minimum--distance detection, mapping received complex samples to the nearest constellation point and returning both the symbol index and the hard decision.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Channel Modeling}
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{generate\_channel.m}: Generates channel impulse responses for three scenarios: (i) mild 3--tap ISI, (ii) severe 5--tap ISI with precursor and postcursor interference, and (iii) random Rayleigh fading channels with configurable length.
\item \texttt{normalize\_channel.m}: Normalizes the channel impulse response to unit energy, ensuring $\sum_n |h[n]|^2 = 1$ so the channel does not amplify or attenuate average signal power.
\item \texttt{pick\_decision\_delay.m}: Selects the optimal decision delay $D$ by aligning the equalizer's center tap with the dominant channel tap, computed as $D = \arg\max_k |h[k]| - 1$ clipped to $[0, N-1]$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Noise and Signal Processing}
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{add\_awgn.m}: Adds complex circularly symmetric additive white Gaussian noise to achieve a target SNR. Computes noise variance as $\sigma_n^2 = \sigma_s^2 / 10^{\text{SNR}_{\text{dB}}/10}$ and generates noise samples $w[n] \sim \mathcal{CN}(0, \sigma_n^2)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Visualization and Metrics}
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{plot\_constellation.m}: Utility function to plot complex scatter diagrams showing received or equalized samples along with the ideal 4--QAM constellation reference points.
\item \texttt{measure\_ser.m}: Computes the symbol error rate by comparing ground truth symbol indices to detected symbol indices, returning the fraction of incorrect decisions.
\end{itemize}
